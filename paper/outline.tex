\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Event cameras have moved from a research curiosity toward commercial deployment in
robotics, automotive systems, and augmented and virtual reality, driven by demands for
low latency and high dynamic range \cite{gallego2022event}. Applications include visual
SLAM and obstacle avoidance for robotic spatial awareness
\cite{vidal2018ultimate}, perception and steering prediction for autonomous driving
\cite{maqueda2018steering}, and low-power, always-on near-eye tracking
\cite{angelopoulos2021gaze}. These are settings in which conventional frame cameras
struggle with fast motion, high latency, or extreme illumination.

Unlike a frame camera, which samples every pixel at a fixed rate, a dynamic vision
sensor (DVS) pixel operates independently and asynchronously. It emits an event when
the change in log intensity exceeds a contrast threshold, producing a stream of
spatiotemporal tuples
\[
    e_k = (x_k,y_k,t_k,p_k),
\]
where \(p_k\) is the polarity of the brightness change
\cite{lichtsteiner2008dvs,gallego2022event}. This change-triggered operation produces
microsecond-scale temporal resolution, high dynamic range, low power consumption, and
an absence of conventional motion blur. At the same time, the sparse asynchronous
stream has neither a fixed sampling rate nor a dense image grid, so it cannot be passed
directly to algorithms designed around conventional frames.

Consequently, event streams are either processed by asynchronous methods or converted
into intermediate representations compatible with standard learning systems. Common
choices include binned event frames \cite{maqueda2018steering}, binary event frames
\cite{barchid2022binarep}, memory and time surfaces
\cite{afshar2019surfaces,lagorce2017hots,sironi2018hats}, and voxel grids
\cite{zhu2019unsupervised}. Most current learning methods built on these
representations are supervised. Their need for large labeled datasets creates a
particularly difficult bottleneck because high-speed event data and corresponding
ground truth are expensive to capture and annotate
\cite{wang2024eyetracking,hu2021v2e,lin2022dvsvoltmeter}.

The cost of collecting real recordings has encouraged the use of synthetic events.
The widely used v2e simulator converts video frames into event streams, using video
interpolation to increase the temporal sampling rate \cite{hu2021v2e}. More recent
simulators target different limitations of this pipeline, including stochastic
sensor-process modeling in DVS-Voltmeter \cite{lin2022dvsvoltmeter}, physically based
simulation in PECS \cite{han2024pecs}, and continuous video-to-event conversion in V2CE
\cite{zhang2024v2ce}. Nevertheless, every simulator introduces a domain gap between
synthetic and real data.

Existing simulators are commonly evaluated qualitatively or through a particular
downstream task rather than through a common direct measurement of event-stream
realism \cite{tan2025carla,chen2024instance}. As a result, realism claims cannot be
compared across methods on equal terms. Learned measures such as the Event Quality
Score begin to address this problem, but depend on the latent features of a pretrained
network rather than on a physically interpretable and directly reproducible
measurement \cite{chanda2025eqs}.

This work proposes a task-independent framework for directly quantifying the gap
between real and simulated event data. It treats raw events as samples from
spatiotemporal point distributions and combines multi-scale Maximum Mean Discrepancy
(MMD) \cite{gretton2012kernel} and Sliced Wasserstein Distance (SWD)
\cite{bonneel2015sliced,flamary2021pot}, with Chamfer distance as a point-set baseline.
The metrics are calibrated through controlled optical-chopper recordings acquired with
a Prophesee EVK4 and paired frame camera. The remainder of the paper introduces the DVS
model and the candidate metrics, defines the common event representation and calibrated
comparison pipeline, and evaluates the metrics before applying them to real--synthetic
comparisons.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Event generation and sensor non-idealities}

In the ideal DVS model, every pixel continuously monitors log intensity
\(L(\bm{x},t)=\log I(\bm{x},t)\). An event is emitted at location \(\bm{x}_k\) and time
\(t_k\) when the change since the previous event crosses the polarity-dependent contrast
threshold:
\begin{equation}
    p_k\!\left[L(\bm{x}_k,t_k)-L(\bm{x}_k,t_k-\Delta t_k)\right]
    \geq C_{p_k},
    \label{eq:event-generation}
\end{equation}
where \(p_k\in\{-1,+1\}\). The pixel then enters a brief refractory period
\cite{lichtsteiner2008dvs,gallego2022event}. This idealized brightness-change detector
is the generative model that both the physical sensor and any faithful simulator seek
to follow.

Real sensors depart from this ideal model. The user does not directly set a physically
interpretable threshold \(C_{p_k}\); instead, device-specific bias currents shift circuit
operating points. Their mapping to the effective threshold depends on factory trim,
temperature, and photocurrent \cite{graca2023biasing}. Pixel-to-pixel threshold
mismatch, intensity-dependent bandwidth and latency, refractory behavior, and
background-activity noise further separate the sensor from the idealized model
\cite{hu2021v2e,graca2021noise}. These departures are precisely the effects that a
simulator attempts to reproduce and that a useful comparison metric must be able to
detect.

\subsection{Event streams as distribution samples}

Reproducing sensor behavior is the simulator's task; evaluating that reproduction
requires a representation that is not tied to a downstream application. An event
recording can be viewed as a set of \((x,y,t,p)\) samples from an underlying distribution
over a space--time volume. Given a real set \(X\) and a synthetic set \(Y\) generated
from the same stimulus, simulator realism becomes a two-sample problem: how different
are the distributions that generated \(X\) and \(Y\)?

MMD answers this question through distribution embeddings in a reproducing-kernel
Hilbert space. Each distribution is represented by the mean of its embedded samples,
and MMD is the distance between the two means \cite{gretton2012kernel}. Wasserstein
distance instead measures the minimum transport cost required to move one distribution
onto the other. SWD makes this practical in higher dimensions by averaging inexpensive
one-dimensional Wasserstein distances over projection directions
\cite{bonneel2015sliced,flamary2021pot}.

Chamfer distance provides a point-level baseline. It matches every point to its nearest
neighbor in the other set and is widely used for point-cloud comparison
\cite{fan2017pointset}. Unlike MMD and SWD, it compares local point positions rather
than distributions. This makes it intuitive, but potentially less robust to event noise,
density, and unmatched points.

Spike-train distances from computational neuroscience are a natural alternative.
The van Rossum distance convolves spike trains with a temporal kernel before comparing
them \cite{vanrossum2001novel}, while SPIKE-distance characterizes differences in
one-dimensional spike timing \cite{kreuz2013monitoring}. Applying such methods at the
event counts of a \(1280\times720\) sensor would be computationally expensive, and their
one-dimensional formulation does not naturally capture the spatial organization that
carries much of the event signal. We therefore focus on point-distribution distances.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Framework overview}
\label{sec:framework}

The framework is a fixed pipeline from paired acquisition to a set of distance values
for each experimental condition. Real and synthetic events are acquired from the same
stimulus, converted into a common polarity-aware point representation, scaled so that
space and time are commensurable, and divided into comparison windows. MMD and SWD are
then computed alongside the Chamfer baseline.

    Placeholder for the paired-acquisition and metric pipeline figure.}}
    \caption{Framework overview from paired acquisition through common event
    representation, windowing, and metric computation.}
    \label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

Paired acquisition is intended to make the residual difference attributable to the
simulator rather than to a change in scene content. A Prophesee EVK4 event camera and an
Allied Vision Mako frame camera view the same stimulus and are externally triggered from
a common source. The Mako frames drive v2e, so the real and synthetic streams originate
from the same physical scene.

The validation stimulus is an optical chopper, chosen because it produces a periodic
signal at a known and controllable frequency. Each trial contains repeated instances of
nominally the same stimulus, which provides real--real comparisons for estimating
measurement variability and a practical noise floor. Sweeping the chopper speed gives
a single controlled physical variable along which metric behavior can be evaluated.
Because the chopper motor is free-running rather than phase-locked to the cameras,
periods are recovered from its reference output rather than inferred from frame count.

Every free parameter is fixed by rule rather than fitted to the pair of datasets being
compared. Kernel bandwidths, projection count, and the space--time conversion are
anchored to sensor geometry, numerical convergence, or application physics. This
restriction is essential if values obtained under different conditions are to remain
comparable.

The measurement is defined for one physical sensor and configuration at a time. Within
that scope, the framework can track one simulator across conditions or rank multiple
simulators against the same recordings. Direct comparison of absolute metric values
across different sensors is outside the present scope because the spatial and temporal
scales are anchored to the geometry and operating conditions of the sensor being
modeled.

\subsection{Event representation}
\label{sec:representation}

Within a comparison window, events are represented as three-dimensional points
\begin{equation}
    \bm{z}_i = \left(x_i,\,y_i,\,t_i/\tau_{\mathrm{px}}\right),
    \label{eq:event-representation}
\end{equation}
where \(\tau_{\mathrm{px}}\) is expressed in microseconds per pixel. ON and OFF events are
split into separate channels, compared independently, and combined only after
channel-level metric computation.

The temporal conversion prevents one coordinate from dominating merely because it is
expressed in different units. We anchor \(\tau_{\mathrm{px}}\) to motion expected in the
target application rather than fitting it to the two distributions. For the eye-motion
case, the saccade main-sequence relation \(D=2.2A+21\), together with the sensor geometry,
implies a range of approximately
\(21\text{--}42\,\mu\mathrm{s/px}\)
\cite{bahill1975mainsequence,carpenter1988movements,gibaldi2021saccade}. The
amplitude--duration form is used because it remains approximately linear over the
amplitudes of interest, whereas peak velocity saturates. Because no single main-sequence
model is universally accepted, sensitivity across this range must be reported.

Comparisons are made in fixed temporal windows rather than over entire recordings. The
window duration and placement rule are held constant across a test, and downstream
metric parameters are constructed to be independent of the particular samples within a
window.

\subsection{Maximum Mean Discrepancy}
\label{sec:mmd}

MMD compares the global structure of two sample sets rather than matching individual
points. For real samples \(X=\{\bm{x}_1,\ldots,\bm{x}_m\}\) and synthetic samples
\(Y=\{\bm{y}_1,\ldots,\bm{y}_n\}\), the unbiased estimator is
\begin{align}
    \widehat{\operatorname{MMD}}_u^2(X,Y)
    ={}&
    \frac{1}{m(m-1)}
    \sum_{\substack{i,j=1\\i\neq j}}^m k(\bm{x}_i,\bm{x}_j)
    +
    \frac{1}{n(n-1)}
    \sum_{\substack{i,j=1\\i\neq j}}^n k(\bm{y}_i,\bm{y}_j)
    \nonumber\\
    &-
    \frac{2}{mn}
    \sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^n k(\bm{x}_i,\bm{y}_j).
    \label{eq:mmd-unbiased}
\end{align}
If the sets follow the same distribution, cross-set similarity approaches within-set
similarity and the terms cancel. If the distributions differ, the within-set terms
dominate.

We use the Gaussian kernel
\begin{equation}
    k(\bm{a},\bm{b})
    =
    \exp\!\left(
    -\frac{\lVert\bm{a}-\bm{b}\rVert_2^2}{2\sigma^2}
    \right),
    \label{eq:rbf-kernel}
\end{equation}
where \(\sigma\) controls the spatial--temporal length scale at which structure is
compared.

The unbiased estimator is required because the biased estimator includes self-pairs,
for which the Gaussian kernel is exactly one. Those diagonal terms introduce a positive
sample-size-dependent offset. Event count varies with sensor noise and simulator
behavior, so this offset would be confounded with the discrepancy being measured
\cite{gretton2012kernel}. Although \cref{eq:mmd-unbiased} is valid for unequal \(m\) and
\(n\), the evaluation protocol subsamples equal numbers of real and synthetic events so
that every metric is compared at a common sample size.

The implementation uses several fixed kernel bandwidths anchored to sensor-scale
structure. This replaces the median heuristic, whose value changes with the samples and
window duration and would therefore break cross-condition comparability. Small
bandwidths resolve fine structure but have an important failure mode under added
uniform noise: most kernel evaluations approach zero while the number of pairs grows,
so the estimated MMD can decrease even though the sets have become more different. The
bandwidth set therefore spans multiple scales, and the response of each kernel is
characterized under controlled perturbations before real--synthetic evaluation.

\subsection{Sliced Wasserstein Distance}
\label{sec:swd}

Wasserstein distance measures the minimum work required to transport the mass of one
distribution onto another. Direct optimal transport in three dimensions is expensive,
but in one dimension the optimal assignment is obtained by sorting and matching
samples. SWD exploits this fact by projecting the point sets onto multiple
one-dimensional directions:
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\operatorname{SW}}_p(X,Y)
    =
    \frac{1}{L}
    \sum_{\ell=1}^{L}
    W_p\!\left(
       \bm{\theta}_{\ell}^{\mathsf T}X,\,
       \bm{\theta}_{\ell}^{\mathsf T}Y
    \right),
    \label{eq:swd}
\end{equation}
where each \(\bm{\theta}_\ell\) is a unit projection direction, \(L\) is the number of
directions, and \(W_p\) is the one-dimensional Wasserstein distance of order \(p\)
\cite{bonneel2015sliced,flamary2021pot}. We use \(p=2\).

The directions are sampled by Monte Carlo, so the reported value is an estimate.
Repeating the computation on identical data produces a residual spread controlled by
\(L\).

\subsection{Chamfer-distance baseline}
\label{sec:chamfer}

For point sets \(X\) and \(Y\), the symmetric Chamfer distance is
\begin{equation}
    d_{\mathrm{CD}}(X,Y)
    =
    \frac{1}{|X|}\sum_{\bm{x}\in X}\min_{\bm{y}\in Y}
        \lVert\bm{x}-\bm{y}\rVert_2
    +
    \frac{1}{|Y|}\sum_{\bm{y}\in Y}\min_{\bm{x}\in X}
        \lVert\bm{y}-\bm{x}\rVert_2.
    \label{eq:chamfer}
\end{equation}
It is evaluated on the same polarity-split representation as MMD and SWD. Its role is
to show what an intuitive nearest-neighbor point-set distance misses relative to
distribution-level metrics, particularly under changes in noise, density, and temporal
structure \cite{fan2017pointset}.
