\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Event cameras have moved from a research curiosity toward practical applications in robotics, automotive systems, and AR/VR, driven by demands for low latency and high dynamic range~\cite{gallego2022event}. In robotics, events have been combined with frames and inertial measurements to keep localization and mapping reliable during fast motion in high-dynamic-range scenes~\cite{vidal2018ultimate}. In autonomous driving, steering angles have been predicted directly from events in difficult lighting~\cite{maqueda2018steering}. In AR/VR applications, events allow near-eye gaze tracking at update rates beyond 10 kHz~\cite{angelopoulos2021gaze}. These are conditions in which conventional frame cameras struggle to keep up within a limited power and hardware budget.


Unlike a conventional camera that captures complete images at a fixed frame rate, a dynamic vision sensor (DVS) reports brightness changes independently at each pixel. When the change in log intensity reaches a threshold, the pixel emits an event containing its coordinates, timestamp, and the polarity of the change~\cite{lichtsteiner2008dvs}. The output is therefore a stream of sparse events, commonly represented as $(x,y,t,p)$, rather than a sequence of dense intensity images.

As a result of this sensor design, event cameras can operate more efficiently and reliably than conventional frame cameras in some conditions, such as capturing fast motion with negligible motion blur, recording under extreme lighting, or running on a mobile platform with limited power and computation~\cite{gallego2022event}. However, preserving these benefits at the system level requires downstream processing that is complementary to the unique characteristics of the event stream. Unlike the dense images that conventional algorithms expect, event data are spatially sparse and temporally asynchronous. Researchers are therefore developing new methods for various downstream tasks, usually based on machine learning methods.

Most current machine learning methods for event data are supervised. Supervised training usually needs large datasets paired with ground truth to measure training loss. But obtaining this ground truth is challenging. Manual annotation can be difficult and time-consuming because events encode local brightness changes rather than complete object appearances, which leaves object boundaries sparse or ambiguous even when events are accumulated into images. Alternatively, labels can be obtained manually or automatically from a synchronized conventional camera and transferred to the event stream through spatial calibration. However, these labels inherit errors from image acquisition, annotation, and alignment issues, and directly observed labels are limited to the frame rate of the camera.

Event-stream simulation offers an alternative or complementary source of training data~\cite{hu2021v2e,lin2022dvsvoltmeter}. A simulator takes videos or rendered 3D scenes as input and models how a sensor would turn their brightness changes into events. v2e remains widely used despite some known limitations~\cite{hu2021v2e}, and later simulators, such as DVS-Voltmeter~\cite{lin2022dvsvoltmeter}, PECS~\cite{han2024pecs}, and V2CE~\cite{zhang2024v2ce}, have improved the output from different perspectives. More details about them will be discussed in Section~\ref{sec:background}.

However, reproducing the response of a real sensor remains difficult. The field is still working toward a physically realistic and computationally efficient model of the event sensor~\cite{graca2025sensormodel}, so a domain gap remains between real and simulated data, which may degrade the performance of models trained on synthetic data~\cite{tan2025carla}. Most existing simulators are validated qualitatively or on a selected set of downstream tasks, rather than by a common, direct comparison with real recordings~\cite{hu2021v2e,lin2022dvsvoltmeter,tan2025carla,chen2024instance}. As a result, claims of realism cannot be compared across simulators on equal footing. The Event Quality Score (EQS) measures the quality of simulated event streams by comparing them with real events in the latent space of a pretrained network, but its value has no unit and is difficult to interpret~\cite{chanda2025eqs}. PECS measured the difference between real and simulated events with Chamfer distance~\cite{han2024pecs}, but because it compares each event only with its closest event in the other stream, its value changes with the number of events as well as with their distribution.

In this study, we present a measurement framework for directly quantifying the difference between real and simulated event streams under controlled conditions, and for characterizing how the resulting distances should be interpreted. The framework combines calibrated, paired recordings of a periodic stimulus with methods that measure differences at the level of distributions, namely maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) and sliced Wasserstein distance (SWD), and with Chamfer distance as an independent verification. We also present a series of experiments that evaluate how these metrics and their parameters respond to artificially imposed forms of dissimilarity between a ground-truth reference event stream and a perturbed, degraded copy. The manipulations include spatial translation, scaling, jitter, temporal offset, subsampling, and uniform noise, and each varies in magnitude. Based on these experiments, we recommend using SWD and MMD together, with SWD guiding the choice of MMD kernel.
% --- new, second part ---
The manipulations are imposed on a novel dataset collected to support this investigation. Repeated observations of a repeating stimulus let us characterize how much real recordings differ under nominally identical conditions. We therefore use an optical chopper, whose adjustable rotation speed and repeating structure support these comparisons across different motion speeds. Its rotation was recorded at five speeds by an event camera spatially aligned with a conventional frame camera, and automated timestamps were recorded to facilitate temporal alignment of the images with the event stream. We then compare real events with events generated by v2e and V2CE from the same frames, and interpret these distances against the differences between repeated real observations and the responses to the imposed manipulations.
