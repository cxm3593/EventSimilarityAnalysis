\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Event cameras have moved from a research curiosity toward practical applications  in robotics, automotive systems, and AR/VR, driven by demands for low latency and high dynamic range~\cite{gallego2022event}. In robotics, events have been combined with frames and inertial measurements to keep localization and mapping reliable during fast motion in high-dynamic-range scenes~\cite{vidal2018ultimate}. In autonomous driving, steering angles have been predicted directly from events in difficult lighting ~\cite{maqueda2018steering}. In AR/VR applications, events allow near-eye gaze tracking at update rates beyond 10 kHz~\cite{angelopoulos2021gaze}. These are conditions in which conventional frame cameras struggle to keep up within a limited power and hardware budget.

Unlike a conventional camera that captures complete images at a fixed frame rate, a dynamic vision sensor (DVS) reports brightness changes independently at each pixel. When the change in log intensity reaches a threshold, the pixel emits an event containing its coordinates, timestamp, and the polarity of the change~\cite{lichtsteiner2008dvs}. The output is therefore a stream of sparse events, commonly represented as $(x,y,t,p)$, rather than a sequence of dense intensity images.

Because each pixel responds to changes independently, event cameras offer microsecond temporal resolution, low latency, high dynamic range, and low power consumption~\cite{gallego2022event}. They also avoid the motion blur caused by exposure time in conventional cameras. However, each pixel responds to brightness changes at a limited speed, which is even slower in dim light, so the events from a fast-moving edge can be delayed and spread out in time~\cite{hu2021v2e}. The same design also changes the form of the output. Events arrive asynchronously and sparsely rather than as dense images at a fixed rate, so they cannot be fed directly to computer vision algorithms built for dense images. 

Machine learning methods are a common way to process event data, either by handling the event stream directly or by first converting it into a representation suitable for a neural network. Many of these methods use supervised learning and therefore require labeled training data. Collecting real events and obtaining accurate labels can be difficult, particularly for fast motion or in conditions where a conventional camera cannot provide reliable ground truth. This limits the available training data and motivates the use of synthetic events~\cite{hu2021v2e,lin2022dvsvoltmeter}.

The cost and difficulty of collecting and labeling real data push the field toward synthetic data generated by event-camera simulators. A simulator takes videos or rendered 3D scenes as input and models how a sensor would turn their brightness changes into events. Simulators differ in which part of this process they model in detail. v2e, a widely used simulator which is still popular today, converts video frames into events with a pixel model that includes limited bandwidth and noise, and interpolates additional frames to improve temporal resolution~\cite{hu2021v2e}. DVS-Voltmeter focuses on the pixel itself, modeling its voltage as a stochastic process so that event timing varies as in a real sensor~\cite{lin2022dvsvoltmeter}. PECS starts from a 3D scene and also simulates how light passes through the lens before reaching the pixels~\cite{han2024pecs}. V2CE converts video into events with a learned model and infers continuous timestamps, rather than placing events at frame times~\cite{zhang2024v2ce}. Because each simulator models a different part of the process in detail, their outputs can differ in both spatial and temporal structure.

However, reproducing the response of a real sensor remains difficult. The field is still working toward a physically realistic and computationally efficient model of the event sensor~\cite{graca2025sensormodel}. Such a model must account for effects such as the sensor circuitry, the light reaching each pixel, and noise, and the quality of synthetic data depends on how well these effects are reproduced. Although several simulators since v2e have addressed known limitations, it remains difficult to tell how much closer their output has come to real data. Simulator evaluations commonly combine visual inspection with performance on selected downstream tasks~\cite{hu2021v2e,lin2022dvsvoltmeter,tan2025carla,chen2024instance}, rather than a common, direct comparison with real recordings. As a result, evaluations of different simulators cannot be compared on equal footing. PECS compared its output with real events using point-set distances~\cite{han2024pecs}, and the Event Quality Score (EQS) addresses this gap by comparing real and simulated events in the latent space of a pretrained network, but its result depends on what that network learned and is difficult to interpret in physical terms~\cite{chanda2025eqs}.

Downstream performance shows how well synthetic data serve a particular task, but does not directly measure how closely they reproduce the information collected from real sensor in general. A direct comparison with recordings of the same stimulus would provided a separate assessment of simulator output and how it changes with parameter settings. To support such an assessment, the recordings must be comparable, and the resulting distances need a reference that helps explain their magnitude and sensitivity. 

We propose a measurement framework for comparing real and simulated event streams under controlled conditions and for interpreting the resulting distances. The framework combines calibrated, paired recordings with maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) and sliced Wasserstein distance (SWD). We also include Chamfer distance, which PECS used to compare events~\cite{han2024pecs}, as an independent verification. Controlled tests show how each distance responds to known changes in the event data, and therefore how its values should be interpreted under the chosen settings. Based on these tests, we recommend using SWD and MMD together, with SWD guiding the choice of MMD kernel. Our main contribution is the calibrated, paired data and the comparison procedure built on them, together with these controlled tests.

A distance reduces the differences between two event streams to a single value, so it cannot describe every way in which they are similar or different. Each measured distance therefore reflects a specific configuration, including the space--time scale, the window length, and the kernel bandwidth. We explain how these parameters are defined and how they affect the results. We also give guidance on choosing them, and an example study showing how the framework is used with a chosen configuration.

Under a chosen configuration, the measurement should respond to differences that arise during data acquisition and event generation. To examine this, we apply controlled perturbations of known magnitude to real recordings. Spatial translation and scaling represent remaining calibration errors, temporal offset represents misalignment in time, and spatial jitter represents small errors in the positions of individual events. Uniform noise adds events unrelated to the stimulus, while subsampling removes events without changing their underlying distribution. Comparing the original and perturbed data shows whether each distance responds to each change and how its response varies with the magnitude. We expect a suitable distance to respond to the first five changes but remain largely insensitive to subsampling.

Comparing real and simulated events requires paired real-event and image recordings of a repeatable stimulus. To obtain such data, we record a rotating optical chopper simultaneously with an event camera and a frame camera. The frame sequence supplies input to video-based simulators, in this study v2e and V2CE, while calibration establishes spatial and temporal alignment with the real events. The chopper provides a simple stimulus whose pattern repeats with every rotation, and we record it at five rotation speeds. Its known geometry and periodic motion also provide references for refining the spatial and temporal alignment. Because the pattern repeats under nominally identical conditions, comparing successive rotations shows how much real recordings differ from one another, which sets a floor for interpreting real--simulated distances.

The remainder of the paper first introduces the sensor model and the comparison methods. The methodology then describes data acquisition, calibration, event representation, and the evaluation protocol. The evaluation presents the controlled tests and simulator comparisons, followed by a discussion of their implications and limitations.
